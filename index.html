State Data Shows That Black Americans Are Imprisoned at Up to 12 Times the Rate of White Americans; Latinx Have a Smaller but Significant Gap

Rachel Mahoney Data Storytelling

As a Black American in New Jersey, you are 12.5 times more likely to be in state prison than a single white resident, the largest racial disparity in America, despite only making up about 15% of the state. Vermont comes at a close second, but with an even wider racial disparity. Although Black residents make up only 2.31% of the population there, the ratio of Black to white inmates is 12.3 to 1.

This trend is not limited to just these two states. According to state data from The Sentencing Project, all 50 states imprison Black residents at more than twice the rate of white residents, and many at even higher rates. Some of the other high-rate states besides the top two are Wisconsin (11.9), Minnesota (9.9), Connecticut (9.4), Iowa (9.3), and California (9.2). Even the state with the smallest gap, Hawaii, still imprisons Black residents at a rate of 2.4 to one.

Latinx residents have a smaller but still significant gap in imprisonment compared with white residents. Many states still imprison Latinx Americans at more than twice the rate of white Americans, and Massachusetts has the largest gap, with four Latinx residents imprisoned for every one white resident. Connecticut (3.7), Maine (3.6), and New York (3.0) follow close behind. Only about half of the United States imprisons Latinx residents at higher rates than white residents.

A 2023 report, also from the Sentencing Project, highlighted policing as a major driving force for these rates. They found that, of the people they pulled over, police officers were more likely to search Black (6.2%) and Latinx (9.2%) drivers than white drivers (3.6%), yet were also less likely to find drugs or weapons when searching a Black or Latinx driver. Further, while white and Black Americans use illegal drugs at similar rates, about one in four people arrested for drug law violations are Black, despite only making up 14% of the United States population. Policing cannot explain everything, however, which the study acknowledges.

The data has some limits. It shows the demographics in prison but doesn't explain why. After completing this story, I also think that comparing each state's Black population with its Black imprisonment rate would be a good angle to include. A state like Vermont, which has such a small Black population but one of the highest imprisonment rates, is an interesting data point that deserves more focus in the future.

Finally, Alabama does not provide any Latinx data, meaning there are only 49 states to work with, and Louisiana also has a figure of zero. There are several limitations, but the data is still a valuable tool to look at prison make-up.

Voter Disenfranchisement Continues to Be on the Rise Within Communities of Color

While most states have not had major surges in voter disenfranchisement, several have still seen spikes in Black and Latinx communities since 2022. I compared the 2024 Locked Out dataset with the last one published, in 2022, to look at how the states have changed. Maine and Vermont do not disenfranchise voters, and other states did not provide any data at all.

Louisiana and North Carolina had the largest Latinx spikes, at more than 1%. North Carolina also had one of the higher Black spikes in disenfranchisement. Minnesota, Nebraska, New Mexico, and New Jersey all saw declines approaching 1% in Black and Latinx voter disenfranchisement.
